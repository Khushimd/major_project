About melanoma

What melanoma is, and how it affects people

Melanoma is a serious form of skin cancer. Understanding the disease, how it develops, and why early detection matters helps patients and clinicians act sooner.

Melanoma begins in melanocytes, the cells that produce melanin — the pigment that gives skin its colour. It often starts as a new mole or as a change in an existing one. Unlike many other skin cancers, melanoma can grow quickly and spread to lymph nodes and other organs if it is not recognised early.

Anyone can develop melanoma. Risk is higher with intense sun or UV exposure, fair skin, many moles, a previous skin cancer, or a family history of melanoma. The impact is not only medical: delayed diagnosis can mean more invasive treatment, greater uncertainty, and a heavier burden on patients and families.

What it is

A cancer of pigment-producing skin cells. It may look like a dark, irregular, or changing spot on the skin, including areas not often exposed to the sun.

How it affects the body

If untreated, melanoma can invade deeper layers of skin and travel through the blood or lymph system. Later-stage disease is harder to treat and can affect organs far from the original lesion.

Warning signs

Watch for the ABCDE signs: Asymmetry, irregular Border, uneven Colour, Diameter larger than a pencil eraser, and Evolving size, shape, or symptoms such as itching or bleeding.

Why early detection matters

Melanoma found early is often treatable. Catching a suspicious lesion during a consultation — before it spreads — can change the course of care and the outcome for the patient.

This information is educational. It is not a diagnosis. A qualified healthcare professional should assess any concerning skin change.

Features

Heatmaps, staging, reports, and appointments

Melanoma CDSS is a student-built clinical decision support project for in-person dermatology visits. It helps the doctor review an image with the patient in the room — then record staging and a report the patient can open later.

The project combines a dermoscopic image model, explainable visual attention, rule-based AJCC staging, PDF report generation, and appointment handling. The doctor remains responsible for clinical judgement. The system does not replace diagnosis or decide treatment on its own.

Heatmap visibility

When the prediction is melanoma, a Grad-CAM heatmap is shown so the doctor can see which regions of the image the model attended to. Heatmaps are hidden for non-melanoma predictions. They illustrate model attention — they are not proof of disease.

AJCC rule-based staging

AJCC stage is calculated from clinical and pathology values entered by the doctor, using published staging rules. The image classifier does not choose the stage. Staging is available when the AI-assisted prediction is melanoma.

PDF reports

After the consultation, the doctor can generate a PDF report with the image, prediction, heatmap (when shown), AJCC stage, and remarks. The patient later opens and downloads that report using the phone number recorded at the visit.

Appointments

Patients can request an in-person appointment. Doctors can confirm, reschedule, complete, or cancel requests from the clinical dashboard — so the analysis happens during a real consultation, not through a virtual waiting list.

More about the project

This Clinical Decision Support System was developed as an academic major project to support safer skin-health assessment. A doctor logs in, starts the visit, uploads a dermoscopic image, reviews the AI-assisted class and probability, inspects the Grad-CAM heatmap when melanoma is predicted, enters clinical details for rule-based AJCC staging, and generates the patient’s PDF report. Patients use a separate login to view only their own reports and to manage appointments.

Role-based access keeps doctor tools (analysis, staging, model analytics) separate from the patient portal. The public landing page does not display internal model scores; those remain inside the authenticated doctor workspace.
